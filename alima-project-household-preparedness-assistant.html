Alima Project: AI Household Preparedness Assistant

John Christian YongcoOctober 7, 202612 min read
Alima ProjectDisaster PreparednessHousehold ResilienceClimate IntelligenceSpatial IntelligencePhilippines

TL;DR

Alima Project is Prixial’s research direction for a Philippines-focused household preparedness assistant. It is designed to help families turn official warnings, local conditions, and their own needs into practical plans for food, water, energy, utilities, health, safety, and care.

Alima would not replace PAGASA, LGUs, barangay officials, responders, or family judgment. Its job is to make trusted information easier to understand and act on before a hazard becomes a crisis.

Through work in GIS data quality, AI-output evaluation, and workflow design, I have learned that information only helps when people can apply it to the situation in front of them. Household preparedness needs the same discipline: trusted sources, local context, clear next steps, and room for human judgment.

The Direct Answer

The Alima Project is not another emergency chatbot. It is a research and product direction for a household preparedness assistant built around local context.

Most families receive disaster information as weather bulletins, posts, forwarded messages, and general checklists. The information may be correct, yet it still leaves hard questions unanswered:

  • Does this apply to our barangay?
  • What should we prepare first?
  • Can we manage without power?
  • What do we do if an older parent needs medicine or a child needs clean water?

Alima is intended to help close that gap. It would connect official information with household-provided details and local references, then organise next steps around the things a family can control.

The aim is simple: help people prepare earlier, with less confusion and less reliance on rumours.

Why Household Preparedness Breaks Down

Preparedness is often treated as a checklist. Buy a flashlight. Store water. Save phone numbers. Know the evacuation route.

Those are sensible steps. But a checklist cannot account for every household’s situation.

One family may depend on a water pump and lose access when power fails. Another may have an infant, an older adult, or a person with limited mobility. A household in a flood-prone sitio may need a different plan from one facing prolonged heat or water shortage. A family that rents a small room cannot prepare in the same way as one with a garage, savings, and a vehicle.

PAGASA’s flood guidance asks families to know the warning system in their community, identify an evacuation area, give members responsibilities, and keep supplies that do not require refrigeration. The point is not merely to have a bag ready. It is to turn a warning into an agreed family plan.

That is where a household assistant can help. It can keep a family’s plan in one place, identify gaps, and explain official information in the context of their resources and needs.

Why This Matters Now

The Philippines is moving from weather descriptions toward impact-based warnings. PAGASA’s Multi-hazard Impact-Based Forecasting and Early Warning System is built around communicating what weather may do, not only what the weather will be. Its purpose is to give at-risk communities time to take action before an event.

There is also evidence that household-focused preparedness tools are becoming a real category. In 2026, PLDT and Smart introduced EVE, a platform for employees and their families that organises household information, identifies possible hazards, and produces personalised emergency plans.

Alima is not a copy of EVE. The signal is that generic safety advice is no longer enough on its own. Families need help translating trusted warnings into decisions that fit their homes, resources, and location.

What Alima Is Designed to Do

Alima begins with a narrow question:

What does this changing condition mean for this household, and what can they do next?

The project would combine four kinds of context:

  • Official information: Forecasts, warnings, public advisories, and local disaster plans
  • Place: Barangay, hazard exposure, evacuation resources, local services, and known infrastructure limits
  • Household needs: Food, water, energy, utilities, health and safety, mobility, and care responsibilities
  • Time: What should be done this week, today, or within the next few hours

Instead of a single generic reply, Alima could help a household build action cards.

Household concernExample questionPossible plan output
WaterCan we manage a three-day interruption?Water estimate, storage check, purification options, and a missing-items list
EnergyWhat happens if we lose power tonight?Phone charging, lighting, medicine, food storage, and contact plan
FoodWhat should we keep before the next advisory?Shelf-stable food list based on household size and cooking limits
CareWho needs help if we evacuate?A private checklist for medicines, mobility, documents, and trusted contacts
UtilitiesWhat should we check before heavy rain?A home and service checklist, with official reporting contacts

These are planning aids, not instructions that override authorities. If an LGU or barangay issues an evacuation order, that order takes priority.

Start With What a Household Can Describe

Alima should not begin by asking families to complete a long government-style form. A profile can grow slowly as people add only information that changes their plan.

Possible categories include:

  • Water supply and storage
  • Food and cooking capacity
  • Power, charging, and lighting
  • Utilities and home conditions
  • Health, medicine, and safety needs
  • Mobility, transport, and evacuation options
  • Family contacts, pets, livestock, or care duties

The system should explain why a question is asked. It should make exact addresses optional where a barangay-level location is enough. It should allow people to correct or delete their information.

This matters because preparedness data is sensitive. A list of older residents, medical needs, or home supplies can create risk if handled carelessly. Alima should collect the minimum needed, limit access, and keep clear retention rules.

Hyperlocal Means More Than a Pin on a Map

Alima’s value depends on context. A map pin is only one part of it.

The same weather warning can mean different things for different households. A family on higher ground may need to prepare for strong winds and power loss. A family near a river may need to prepare documents, transport, and a route to a safe place. During El Niño or a dry period, another household may need to track water use, heat exposure, food costs, and local water schedules.

This is why Alima’s first research focus is household resilience, not an all-disasters promise. It can start with one location, one hazard pattern, and one useful planning window.

For Prixial, El Niño and dry-season readiness are an appropriate first area to study. Water, food, energy, and utilities are daily household systems. They are also the systems people feel first when conditions change.

Source-Backed Answers, Not Confident Guesses

A preparedness assistant earns trust by showing its work.

Each answer should tell the household:

  • What official source it used
  • When that source was published or updated
  • Which part of the answer is a confirmed fact
  • Which part is a location-based estimate
  • What the household should verify with local officials

If the system does not know whether a road is passable, a shelter is open, or water is available, it should say that. It should point people to the right official contact or local channel instead of guessing.

This is the difference between intelligence that only answers and intelligence that carries context into action.

A Calm Interface for Stressful Moments

Preparedness tools should not create more anxiety. Alima should avoid panic language, red-alert visuals, and long blocks of technical text.

The experience should be simple:

  1. See the current condition and its source.
  2. Understand what it may mean for the household.
  3. See the next few actions in order.
  4. Check what is already ready and what is missing.
  5. Find the official contact or local instruction when needed.

Important outputs should work beyond a chat screen. Families may need a printable plan, a saved phone page, a text-friendly summary, or a version that can be shared with a trusted relative. Connectivity often becomes less reliable at the moment information matters most.

A Focused First Pilot

Alima does not need to begin as a national platform. A better first step is a controlled pilot in one local setting.

The pilot could focus on one question:

How can households prepare for a likely water, heat, or utility disruption before it becomes urgent?

It would need:

  1. A selected community and a local partner.
  2. Verified information from PAGASA, the LGU, and barangay disaster plans.
  3. A small set of voluntary household profiles.
  4. Short action cards for water, food, energy, utilities, and care.
  5. A drill or feedback session to test whether people understand and can use the plan.

The test is not whether the tool gives impressive answers. The test is whether households can find the right next step quickly, understand the source, and spot what they need to prepare.

What Alima Will Not Claim to Do

Alima should not claim to predict whether a specific home will be damaged. It should not promise that a family is safe. It should not replace emergency services, official advisories, local disaster plans, or the judgment of people on the ground.

It should also not pretend every household has the same capacity to prepare. A plan must work with real limits: cost, housing conditions, disability, age, transport, literacy, signal access, and available support.

Its role is narrower and more useful: organise reliable information, make household conditions visible, and help families take practical action earlier.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Alima already a public emergency app?

No. Alima is a Prixial research and product project. This article describes its intended direction and the standards it should meet before wider use.

Will Alima replace PAGASA or local government instructions?

No. Official advisories and instructions from authorised local officials remain the source for warnings, evacuation, and emergency response. Alima is meant to help households understand and prepare for those instructions.

What is Alima’s first focus?

Its first research focus is household readiness for conditions that affect water, food, energy, utilities, and care, with El Niño and dry-season readiness as an early use case.

Why not make a general disaster chatbot?

General answers often miss the factors that change a family’s real options. Alima is being designed around household resources, location, timing, trusted sources, and local support.

For Prixial, Alima is an exploration of resilient intelligence: helping households understand changing conditions, decide with better context, and prepare before disruption becomes a crisis.

What This Means

The Alima Project starts with a simple belief: preparedness is personal, but it is not private. Families need plans that fit their own homes, and they also need trusted public information, local support, and clear guidance from authorities.

Prixial’s role is to research how context-aware AI can make that connection easier. Not by acting certain when it is not, but by helping households see what matters, prepare for what they can, and know when to follow official direction.
